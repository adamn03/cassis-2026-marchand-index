Who draws more attention than their play explains?

Some players are famous out of proportion to how well they play. That is measurable. This finds each player’s ten closest comparables in the league and asks how much more public attention he generates than they do — then asks whether anyone pays for the difference.

It is a residual. Not a ranking of who is famous, and not a ranking of who is good. It is what is left over after you subtract the attention a player’s skill-matched peers already generate. A player scores high only by being far more visible than players who produce what he produces.

Two numbers are published. Marchand Index is that residual on its own. Marchand Index, salary adjusted divides it by the player’s cap hit, which asks a different question — not “who is over-noticed” but “who is over-noticed per dollar.” The two lists are not the same, and the gap between them is one of the findings.

The result

Where players land

Peers are matched on what a player did in 2025–26: scoring rate, minutes, situation, even-strength play-driving, shot and chance creation. Attention is then compared against the average of his ten closest comparables.

Why tiers and not a numbered list

A ranking implies the metric can tell #7 from #8. It cannot. Rebuild the peer group using a different but equally defensible set of statistics and a player moves by 0.45 standard deviations on average — about 2.4× more than the sampling error in the attention data itself. Roughly a third of any top 25 changes.

So the tiers below are sized to that uncertainty: wide enough that a player in one tier is genuinely separated from a player in the next, at 95% confidence. Between tiers is a claim. Inside a tier, the order is not, which is why no order is shown.

The bottom tier is nearly empty, and that is the measure rather than the league. A player can be unboundedly above his peers — the most-discussed players in the sport are matched against ordinary ones, so the gap runs a long way. He cannot be far below them, because a quiet player’s skill-matched comparables are quiet too and there is little room underneath. The residual is right-skewed as a result: the 1st percentile sits at −0.92 while the 99th reaches +1.89. Separately, the players who genuinely would land far below — those who missed most of the season, whose attention is floored by absence rather than by obscurity — are held out of every published table on purpose.

What came out of it

Findings

Scope. These are associations measured on one season of observational data, at the level of individual players. They do not establish cause, and confirming any of them takes data this project does not hold. That is stated on each finding rather than left implied.

The index tracks a following it never looked at

The attention measure is built from Wikipedia, Reddit and Google searches. It was never shown a single follower count. Checked afterwards against the larger of each player's Instagram and X followings, it lines up closely.

The correlation falls as more is subtracted, which is the direction it should fall. A raw attention total tracks raw following closely; a residual should not, because the part explained by production has been removed. That the residual still holds a positive relationship means it is measuring public interest rather than statistical leftovers.

What it meansAn independent, off-platform measure of public following agrees with the index. Follower counts are deliberately kept out of the index itself: a follower total is a lifetime stock and the index measures one season of flow. Keeping them out is what makes them usable as a check.
LimitsFollowers were available for — of the published players. Accounts are self-selected, so a player who does not use social media is not a player nobody follows.

The NHL does not pay extra for attention

In a next-contract model fit over 545 signings, a veteran re-signing who generates one standard deviation more attention than his peers earns essentially nothing extra. The interval is narrow enough to exclude a meaningful premium, which makes this a bounded null rather than a shortage of evidence.

What it meansAttention is a cost the market does not charge for. A club acquiring a high-salience player receives the public interest without paying a premium. The same result gives an agent a concrete number to argue against, and tells a front office that salience is one of the few attributes still available at no markup.
LimitsThis is the one result here that must cross seasons, because a next contract is by definition signed in a later one. It also cannot separate clubs leaving money on the table from attention being genuinely worth little to a single club, since much of the revenue it generates accrues to the league rather than the team.

Dividing by salary changes almost everyone

The two published figures disagree about who matters. On the residual alone the top ten is made of superstars on expensive contracts. Divided by salary, the median cap hit of the top ten falls by roughly two thirds and the list fills with veterans and agitators on cheap deals. The cheap half of that second list is a denominator effect, not a discovery about playing style. Tested directly, the residual has no relationship with penalty minutes (ρ = 0.13) or hits (ρ = 0.01). The index does not find agitators. Dividing by a small salary does.

What it meansTwo different questions produce two different answers, and the choice between them has to be made deliberately. “Who is most over-noticed” and “who is most over-noticed per dollar” share only half their top ten. The players in both are the rare case: large in absolute attention and cheap relative to it.
LimitsThat overlap is a small enough set that one retirement or one contract changes it.

How much the ranking depends on who counts as a peer

The peer definition was rebuilt twice against unrelated objections: once with six further measures of this season's play, once against career history — games, points, seasons and draft position. Each replaces roughly half of every player's comparison group. The metric largely survives both, but not exactly.

The career-history rebuild is reported as a test and is not published as a ranking. Comparing a 21-year-old against players with the same career behind him works only where such players exist, and at the edges of a career they do not.

These two figures are why this page shows tiers instead of positions. Correlations of 0.91 and 0.89 mean the broad picture holds — the same players sit near the top under any of the three definitions — while an individual position does not survive the change. The gap between those two facts is exactly the width of a tier.

What it meansThe first objection to any residual is that it reflects the comparison group rather than the player. Two unrelated redefinitions landing at 0.91 and 0.89, while each discards about half the comparison group, is the evidence against that — and the evidence for reporting bands rather than ranks. It also disposes of the specific charge that the index measures accumulated fame: controlling for career length, games played and draft pedigree does not move it.
LimitsBoth rebuilds share the same matching machinery. A different method entirely — propensity weighting, or a synthetic control — would test more than a third list of measures.

How it works

Methods

Everything here is public data, collected free, rate-limited and cached. No paid sources, no private data, nothing behind a login.

Where attention comes from

  • Wikipedia pageviews — English and other language editions, counted separately. Closest thing to neutral curiosity: people looking a player up.
  • Reddit — submissions mentioning the player and the votes those submissions received, across team and league subreddits. Fan conversation rather than lookup.
  • Google Trends — search interest, measured against a fixed reference player so the numbers are comparable across people.

Those three sources give five measures, and every one is counted over the same fixed 365-day window ending the last day of the regular season — so nobody gets credit for a playoff run their peers never had a chance at, and no measure covers a longer stretch of a career than another.

Where skill comes from

  • NHL public API — age, games, points per game, ice time.
  • MoneyPuck — even-strength play-driving and deployment: shot share, expected-goal share, where a player's shifts start, and the individual rate stats used by vector A.
  • CapWages — contract value and type, including whether a deal is entry-level.

Finding a player's peers

For each player, the ten most similar players are selected from the same position class — centres with centres, wingers with wingers, defencemen with defencemen. A centre and a winger with identical statistics are not interchangeable, because role drives coverage in a way the numbers do not capture.

Attention is then compared against the average of those ten. That difference is the Marchand Index. No adjustment is made for the size of the market a player happens to play in — see the limits below for why not.

How the index is checked

Each player's Instagram and X follower counts are collected separately and held out of the index entirely. They are used only afterwards, to test whether a measure built from Wikipedia, Reddit and search behaviour lines up with a public following recorded somewhere else. It does.

Follower totals are kept out of the index on purpose. A follower count is a lifetime accumulation, while everything the index measures is one season of activity, and mixing the two would let a long-retired reputation stand in for current attention. Staying out is also what makes them a usable check rather than a circular one.

Terms, in plain language

Residual
What is left over after subtracting what you expected. Here: a player's actual attention minus the attention his ten comparables generate.
Mahalanobis distance
A way of measuring “how similar are these two players” that accounts for the fact that some statistics move together. Points and ice time rise together, so a player who is high on both is less unusual than counting each separately would suggest. This stops correlated statistics from being double-counted.
Bootstrap interval
The intervals here are built by re-running the whole calculation a thousand times on resampled data and reading off the middle 95% of answers. Days are resampled in week-long blocks, so a single news cycle cannot masquerade as a stable signal.
Covariance shrinkage
With twelve statistics that overlap heavily, the similarity calculation becomes unstable and small quirks in the data get amplified. Shrinkage pulls it gently toward a simpler, steadier version.

Limits

What this does not claim

Attention is a proxy, not revenue

No figure on this page is a club's income. The link between public attention and money is assumed, not measured.

Nothing here is causal

These are conditional associations in observational data. No player was assigned to be famous.

Only the tails mean anything

Under a different but equally defensible peer definition, mid-table players move by hundreds of positions while the metric itself holds at 0.96. Being in the top or bottom handful is a claim. “Ranked 340th” is not, and should never be quoted.

Goalies are excluded

Peer matching breaks down for them — there are too few, and their statistics do not compare to skaters'.

Market size is real and is not corrected for

A player in a large market almost certainly draws attention a player in a small one does not. The correction that was meant to remove it has been dropped, because these sources cannot measure it in a way worth trusting.

Measured across 170 players who changed clubs, moving to a market one standard deviation larger is worth +4.4% more attention, with an interval of [−2.1%, +10.8%] that includes zero. The sources also disagree about the direction: Wikipedia says +15.8% and Reddit mentions say −7.9%, each individually significant. A correction built on sources that disagree about the sign would move every published number without being defensible, so none is applied. The effect is left uncontrolled and declared rather than estimated badly.

Absence is not obscurity

Players who missed most of the season have attention floored by not playing while their skill is estimated from too little evidence. They are held out of every published table rather than shown as if the number meant something.
